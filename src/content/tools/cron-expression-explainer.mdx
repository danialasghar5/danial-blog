The grammar is five fields separated by spaces: minute, hour, day of month, month and day of week. Most scheduling mistakes come from one of two places: the day rule, which is not what most people expect, and the time zone, which decides when "5 AM" actually happens. This guide covers both, then shows how the same schedule is written for Solid Queue and sidekiq-cron.

## How the grammar works

Each field accepts the same building blocks:

- `*` means every value in the field's range.
- A list separates values with commas: `1,15`.
- A range uses a hyphen: `9-17`.
- A step divides a range: `*/15` means every 15 units from the field's start, and `10-20/5` means 10, 15 and 20.
- Months and weekdays also accept three-letter names in any case, such as `JAN` or `mon`.

| Field | Allowed values | Names |
|---|---|---|
| Minute | 0 to 59 | none |
| Hour | 0 to 23 | none |
| Day of month | 1 to 31 | none |
| Month | 1 to 12 | JAN to DEC |
| Day of week | 0 to 7, where 0 and 7 are Sunday | SUN to SAT |

Macros replace the whole expression: `@hourly`, `@daily` (also `@midnight`), `@weekly` (Sunday at midnight), `@monthly` and `@yearly` (also `@annually`). Macros have no seconds field.

Sidekiq-cron and Fugit also accept a six-field form with seconds first, so `*/15 * * * * *` runs every 15 seconds. Standard system cron does not, and the tool treats a six-field expression as seconds-first only.

## The day-of-month and day-of-week rule

This is the rule that surprises people. If you restrict both the day-of-month and the day-of-week fields, a run happens when either one matches. The expression `30 4 1,15 * 5` runs at 04:30 on the 1st, on the 15th and on every Friday. It does not run only on Fridays that fall on the 1st or the 15th.

A day field that starts with `*` counts as unrestricted, so `0 0 */2 * 1` runs only when both conditions hold: a Monday that is also on an odd-numbered day. The tool follows this convention, which matches Vixie cron, and its description names the rule it applied.

## Time zones and daylight saving

The tool evaluates an expression in one zone. That is the selected zone, unless the expression ends with a zone name, as in `0 5 * * * America/New_York`. The zone name wins. Wall-clock times are converted to UTC with the browser's own time zone data, so the answer depends on the rules in force for that year.

Two cases in each year break simple arithmetic:

- **Spring forward.** A wall-clock time inside the gap does not exist. A job set for 02:30 in New York does not run on the night clocks move from 02:00 to 03:00 on 8 March 2026. The tool skips it.
- **Fall back.** A wall-clock time that happens twice runs once. On 1 November 2026, `30 1 * * *` in New York runs at 01:30 EDT, which is 05:30 UTC, and not again at 01:30 EST. This matches Vixie cron.

If your application's zone differs from the zone you meant, state it in the expression. A schedule without a zone follows whatever zone the scheduler uses.

## Solid Queue: recurring.yml and natural language

Solid Queue reads `config/recurring.yml`. Each task has a key, a job `class` (or a `command`), optional `queue`, `priority` and `args`, and a `schedule`. The generator produces this shape for `0 3 * * *`:

```yaml
production:
  nightly_cleanup:
    class: CleanupJob
    queue: background
    args: [ 1000 ]
    schedule: "0 3 * * *"
```

The `schedule` string goes to Fugit. Fugit accepts cron, and it also accepts a small English vocabulary. The tool converts the documented forms, including `every day at 5 pm`, `every tuesday and monday at 5pm`, `every weekday at five`, `every 15s` and `every 4 months`. Phrases outside that subset are reported as unsupported rather than guessed. Paste an English phrase in the expression box and the cron equivalent appears; the YAML then carries the cron text, which Fugit reads the same way.

For how jobs are claimed and run once a schedule fires, read [how Solid Queue works](/blog/how-solid-queue-works/). For the choice between the two backends, read [Solid Queue vs Sidekiq](/blog/solid-queue-vs-sidekiq/).

## sidekiq-cron

sidekiq-cron stores its schedule as a hash keyed by job name, with `cron`, `class`, `queue` and `args`. The generator writes that form as well. The cron string is parsed with Fugit, so the six-field form also works there.

## A worked example

Take `0 5 * * 1-5 America/New_York`. It means 05:00 Eastern on weekdays. Starting from 10:17 UTC on Thursday 26 November 2026, the next run is Friday 27 November at 10:00 UTC, which is 05:00 Eastern Standard Time. The tool shows the list of the next ten runs in the zone you select, and writes the UTC instant beside each one so you can compare it with your logs.

## Limitations

- Fugit extensions are not evaluated. The `&` (AND of the day fields), `#` (nth weekday, such as `mon#2`), `%`, `~` and `L` are detected and rejected with a clear message. The tool does not guess what they would mean.
- The English subset is the documented one. Anything else shows "Not supported here", with a link to Fugit's natural-language notes.
- Next runs look five years ahead. A schedule that never fires in that window, such as `0 0 30 2 *`, shows no run.
- The tool does not support Quartz-style `?` or a year field.
- The results are a calculation. Confirm a new schedule against a real job run before you rely on it for billing or compliance.
